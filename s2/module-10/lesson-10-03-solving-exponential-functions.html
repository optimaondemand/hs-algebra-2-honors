📚 VOCABULARY

Approximate Solution: A value that is close to the exact answer and is often found using graphs, tables, or estimation methods when an exact solution is difficult to determine.

Compound Interest: Interest calculated on the initial amount of money as well as on the accumulated interest from previous compounding periods.

Continuous Growth: Growth that occurs constantly over time and is modeled using an exponential function with base e.

Continuously Compounded Interest: Interest that is added an infinite number of times per year, resulting in exponential growth modeled by the natural base e.

Exponential Function: A function in which the variable appears in the exponent and represents growth or decay at a constant percent rate.

Growth Factor: The multiplier in an exponential function that represents how much a quantity changes for each unit increase of the independent variable, often written as \(1+r\).

Initial Value: The starting amount of a quantity, typically the value of the function when the input is zero.

Interest Rate: The percent of the principal that is earned or charged over a given time period, usually expressed as a decimal or percentage.

Natural Base (e): An irrational number approximately equal to 2.718 that is used as the base of exponential functions modeling continuous growth or decay.

Rate of Growth: The constant percent by which a quantity increases during each time interval in an exponential model.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Exponential functions are used to model situations where quantities grow or decay by a constant percent rather than a constant amount. These models appear in many real-world contexts, such as population growth, radioactive decay, and financial investments. In this lesson, you will learn how to solve exponential models using equations and graphs, interpret solutions in context, and apply exponential functions to compound and continuously compounded interest. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to analyze and compare exponential growth models and explain what their solutions mean in real-world situations.

Solving Mathematical Problems Modeled by Exponential Functions

An exponential function is often written in the form

\(\Large f(x)=a(b)^x\)

where:

  • \(a\) is the initial value,

  • \(b\) is the growth or decay factor,

  • \(x\) is the independent variable.

Sometimes, exponential equations cannot be solved easily using algebra alone. In these cases, graphs are used to approximate input or output values. Solving an exponential model means finding the value of \(x\) or \(y\) that satisfies a given condition.


Example

Given the function \(f(x)=50(1.05)^x\), estimate the input when the function reaches 100.

Using the graph of the function, we find the x value when the function is 100. So, we trace up the y-axis to 100, then trace over to the graph of the function. We find this is approximately where x=9.

We can use the equation to verify our estimation by substituting 100 in for f(x) and solving for x.

Once we isolate the exponential part of the function, we will use logs to solve the function for x.

We find that x is approximately 9 as seen in the graph.

Answer: When the function is 100, x is approximately 9.

Real-world exponential problems involve situations where a quantity increases or decreases by a constant percent over equal time intervals.

  • Decay occurs when the base is between 0 and 1.

Solving these problems involves identifying the initial value, growth or decay rate, and interpreting the result in context.
