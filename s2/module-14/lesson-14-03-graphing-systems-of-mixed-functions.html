📚 VOCABULARY

Absolute Value Function: A function of the form \( f(x) = |x| \) or transformations of it, producing a V-shaped graph whose outputs are always non-negative.

Cubic Function: A polynomial function of degree three, written in the form \( f(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d \), whose graph produces an S-shaped curve.

Cube Root Function: A function of the form \( f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x} \), which returns the number whose cube equals \( x \); its graph passes through the origin and extends in both directions.

Intersection Point: A point where two graphs cross or touch, representing a value of \( x \) for which both equations produce the same output \( y \); each intersection is a solution to the system.

Linear Equation: An equation whose graph is a straight line, typically written as \( y = mx + b \) or in standard form \( Ax + By = C \).

Nonlinear Equation: An equation whose graph is not a straight line; in this lesson, nonlinear equations include quadratic, cubic, square root, cube root, and absolute value functions.

Quadratic Function: A polynomial function of degree two, written in the form \( f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c \), whose graph is a parabola.

Solution Set: The collection of all ordered pairs \( (x, y) \) that satisfy both equations in a system simultaneously; graphically, this corresponds to the set of all intersection points.

Square Root Function: A function of the form \( f(x) = \sqrt{x} \), which returns the non-negative square root of \( x \); its graph begins at the origin and curves upward to the right.

System of Equations: A set of two or more equations considered together, for which a solution must satisfy all equations simultaneously.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In this lesson, you will explore what happens when a straight line and a curved graph — such as a parabola, cubic, square root, cube root, or absolute value function — are graphed together on the same coordinate plane. Where those two graphs cross is where both equations share the same \( x \)-values and \( y \)-values at the same time, and that shared point is the solution to the system. You will use Desmos, a free online graphing tool, to graph these systems, locate the intersection points, and interpret what those points mean. By the end of this lesson, you will be able to graph any system consisting of a linear and nonlinear equation, read off the solution(s) from the graph, verify them algebraically, and apply this skill to real-world situations.

What is a System of Equations - What is a Solution?

A system of equations is simply two equations considered at the same time. Each equation, on its own, describes a relationship between \( x \) and \( y \). When we place both graphs on the same axes, any point where the two graphs intersect is a point that satisfies both equations simultaneously. That shared point — called an intersection point — is the solution to the system.

A solution is written as an ordered pair \( (x, y) \). The \( x \)-value tells you the input at which both equations produce the same output, and the \( y \)-value is that common output. Depending on the shapes of the two graphs, a system can have zero, one, or more than one solution. For example, a line can cross a parabola at two points, touch it at exactly one point, or miss it entirely.


Key Idea — Intersection Points as Solutions

If the point \( (a, b) \) is an intersection of the two graphs, then substituting \( x = a \) into either equation gives \( y = b \). This means \( (a, b) \) satisfies both equations at the same time — it is a solution to the system.

To verify a solution, substitute the ordered pair into each original equation separately and confirm that both produce true statements.

Using Desmos to Graph and Solve a System

Desmos is a free, browser-based graphing calculator available at desmos.com/calculator. You do not need to create an account to use it. To graph a system of a linear and nonlinear equation, you will enter each equation on its own line in the left-hand panel, and Desmos will draw both graphs instantly. You then use Desmos's built-in intersection tool to locate and read off the solution(s).

How to Graph a System and Find Intersection Points in Desmos

  1. Go to desmos.com/calculator in any browser.
  2. Click on the first input line in the left panel and type the first equation (e.g., y=x+1). Press Enter to move to the next line.
  3. Type the second equation on the new line (e.g., y=x^2-3). Both graphs will appear automatically on the grid.
  4. To find the intersection: click directly on one of the intersection points on the graph. Desmos will display the coordinates of that point in a small label.
  5. If the intersection is hard to click precisely, zoom in using the scroll wheel or the + button, then click the point again.
  6. Read the ordered pair \( (x, y) \) shown by Desmos. That is your solution. If there are two intersection points, click each one to record both solutions.
  7. Verify by substituting the coordinates back into both original equations by hand.

Desmos Notation Tips

Use ^ for exponents, hold shift and type 6:   \( x^{2} \) is typed as x^2,   \( x^{3} \) is typed as x^3.

For square root, type sqrt(x) or use the function menu.   For cube root, type cbrt(x).

For absolute value, type abs(x) or use the \( | \space | \) button in the keypad.

Desmos accepts equations in any form — you do not need to isolate \( y \) first.



Solve the System

Solve the system \( \begin{cases} y = 2x - 1 \\ y = x^{2} - 2x + 2 \\ \end{cases} \) graphically using Desmos, then verify.

Solution:

Step 1: Enter \( y = 2x - 1 \) on Line 1 and \( y = x^{2} - 2x + 2 \) on Line 2 in Desmos.

Step 2: Observe that the line and the parabola intersect at two points. Click each intersection point to read the coordinates. Desmos displays \( (1, 1) \) and \( (3, 5) \).

Step 3 — Verify \( (1, 1) \):

Equation 1:   \( y = 2(1) - 1 = 1 \) ✓

Equation 2:   \( y = (1)^{2} - 2(1) + 2 = 1 - 2 + 2 = 1 \) ✓

Step 4 — Verify \( (3, 5) \):

Equation 1:   \( y = 2(3) - 1 = 5 \) ✓

Equation 2:   \( y = (3)^{2} - 2(3) + 2 = 9 - 6 + 2 = 5 \) ✓

Solutions:   \( \{(1, 1),(3, 5)\} \) — both verified in both equations.

How Many Solutions Can a Linear-Nonlinear System Have?

Quadratic (parabola): A line can intersect a parabola at \( 0 \), \( 1 \), or \( 2 \) points.

Cubic: A line can intersect a cubic curve at \( 1 \), \( 2 \), or \( 3 \) points.

Square root or cube root: A line will typically intersect these curves at \( 0 \) or \( 1 \) point, though some positions yield \( 2 \) intersections depending on slope and position.

Absolute value: A line can intersect a V-shaped absolute value graph at \( 0 \), \( 1 \), or \( 2 \) points.

The graph is the fastest way to see how many solutions exist before doing any calculation.

Example 1

Use Desmos to graph the system below. Identify all intersection points, state the solution(s) as ordered pairs, and verify each solution in both equations.

\( \begin{cases} y = x + 2 \\ y = x^{2} - 4 \\ \end{cases} \)

Hint: Enter each equation on its own line in Desmos, then click the intersection point(s) to read the coordinates.

Show Solution

Step 1: Enter \( y = x + 2 \) on Line 1 and \( y = x^{2} - 4 \) on Line 2 in Desmos. A line and an upward-opening parabola appear. They cross at two points.

Step 2: Click each intersection. Desmos displays the points \( (-2, 0) \) and \( (3, 5) \).

Step 3 — Verify \( (-2, 0) \):

Equation 1:   \( y = -2 + 2 = 0 \) ✓

Equation 2:   \( y = (-2)^{2} - 4 = 4 - 4 = 0 \) ✓

Step 4 — Verify \( (3, 5) \):

Equation 1:   \( y = 3 + 2 = 5 \) ✓

Equation 2:   \( y = (3)^{2} - 4 = 9 - 4 = 5 \) ✓

Answers:   \( \{(-2, 0),(3, 5)\} \) — two intersection points, both verified.



Example 2

Use Desmos to graph the system below. Identify all intersection points, state the solution(s) as ordered pairs, and verify each solution in both equations.

\(\begin{cases} y = -x + 4 \\ y = |x - 1| \\ \end{cases}\)

Hint: In Desmos, type abs(x-1) or use the absolute value button in the keypad for \( |x - 1| \).

Show Solution

Step 1: Enter \( y = -x + 4 \) on Line 1 and \( y = |x - 1| \) on Line 2 (typed as abs(x-1)). A line with negative slope and a V-shaped graph appear.

Step 2: Notice that the line has slope \( -1 \) and the left branch of \( y = |x - 1| \) also has slope \( -1 \). Because they are parallel, the line will not intersect the left branch. Solve each case algebraically to confirm.

Case 1 — Right branch \( (x \geq 1) \):   \( -x + 4 = x - 1 \Rightarrow 5 = 2x \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{5}{2} \)

\( y = -\dfrac{5}{2} + 4 = \dfrac{3}{2} \)   →   Point: \( \left(\dfrac{5}{2}, \dfrac{3}{2}\right) \)

Case 2 — Left branch \( (x < 1) \):   \( -x + 4 = -(x - 1) = -x + 1 \Rightarrow 4 = 1 \) — false. No solution on the left branch.

Step 3 — Verify \( \left(\dfrac{5}{2}, \dfrac{3}{2}\right) \):

Equation 1:   \( y = -\dfrac{5}{2} + 4 = \dfrac{3}{2} \) ✓

Equation 2:   \( y = \left|\dfrac{5}{2} - 1\right| = \left|\dfrac{3}{2}\right| = \dfrac{3}{2} \) ✓

Answer:   \( \left(\dfrac{5}{2}, \dfrac{3}{2}\right) \) — one intersection point, verified. The line is parallel to the left branch of \( y = |x - 1| \), so it intersects only once.



Applying Linear-Nonlinear Systems to Real-World Problems

Systems of linear and nonlinear equations appear naturally in situations where one quantity changes at a constant rate (linear) while another changes at an increasing or irregular rate (nonlinear). Common examples include comparing a flat-rate cost against a growing revenue curve, a projectile's height meeting a ramp's slope, or the distance traveled by two vehicles following different speed models. When graphing a real-world system, the intersection point tells you the moment when two quantities are equal — and the context tells you what that equality means in the situation.

After finding the intersection on a Desmos graph, always interpret both coordinates in context: state what the \( x \)-value represents (often time, quantity, or distance), what the \( y \)-value represents (often cost, height, or revenue), and include the appropriate units. Also consider whether the solution is reasonable — for example, negative time or a negative number of items sold would not make sense in most real-world situations.

Example 3

A skateboarder launches off a ramp. The height of the ramp above the ground (in feet) at a horizontal distance of \( x \) feet from the launch point is modeled by the linear equation below. After launching, the skateboarder's height follows the quadratic path shown.

\( y = 0.5x + 2 \)     (Ramp surface)

\( y = -0.2x^{2} + 2x + 2 \)     (Skateboarder's flight path)

Use Desmos to graph both equations and find where the skateboarder's flight path meets the ramp surface again after launch. State the solution and interpret it in context. Consider only \( x \geq 0 \).

Show Solution

Step 1: Enter both equations in Desmos. The line and the downward-opening parabola both pass through \( (0, 2) \) — confirming the skateboarder begins on the ramp at a height of \( 2 \) feet. The graphs intersect again to the right of the origin.

Step 2: Click the second intersection point (the one with \( x > 0 \)). Desmos displays \( (7.5, 5.75) \).

Step 3 — Verify \( (7.5, 5.75) \):

Equation 1:   \( y = 0.5(7.5) + 2 = 3.75 + 2 = 5.75 \) ✓

Equation 2:   \( y = -0.2(7.5)^{2} + 2(7.5) + 2 = -0.2(56.25) + 15 + 2 = -11.25 + 17 = 5.75 \) ✓

Both verify. The solution \( x = 0 \) is the launch point (also an intersection); the meaningful answer for "meets the ramp again" is the second point at \( x = 7.5 \).

Solution:   \( (7.5, 5.75) \)

Interpretation: The skateboarder's flight path meets the ramp surface again at a horizontal distance of 7.5 feet from the launch point, at a height of 5.75 feet above the ground. This is the landing point where the skateboarder returns to the ramp after the jump. The solution is physically meaningful — both \( x \) and \( y \) are positive.



Example 4

A new streaming service is tracking two quantities over the first 10 months since launch, where \( t \) is the number of months since launch. The monthly operating cost (in thousands of dollars) grows linearly, while the monthly revenue (in thousands of dollars) follows a square root growth model as the subscriber base builds slowly at first.

\( C = 2t + 3 \)     (Monthly operating cost, in thousands of dollars)

\( R = 5\sqrt{t} + 1 \)     (Monthly revenue, in thousands of dollars)

Use Desmos to graph both equations for \( t \geq 0 \). Find the month at which revenue equals cost (the break-even point), and interpret the solution in context. Round to the nearest tenth if necessary.

Hint: In Desmos, type the revenue equation as y = 5*sqrt(x) + 1 using x in place of \( t \).

Show Solution

Step 1: Enter \( y = 2x + 3 \) (cost) and \( y = 5\sqrt{x} + 1 \) (revenue) in Desmos. The linear cost line rises steadily. The revenue curve starts low, rises steeply at first, then levels off relative to the line. They intersect at one point in the positive domain.

Step 2: Click the intersection point. Desmos displays approximately \( (2.753, 8.506) \). Rounded to the nearest tenth: \( t \approx 2.8 \) months, \( y \approx 8.6 \) thousand dollars.

Step 3 — Verify \( t \approx 2.8 \):

Cost:   \( C = 2(2.8) + 3 = 5.6 + 3 = 8.6 \) thousand dollars ✓

Revenue:   \( R = 5\sqrt{2.8} + 1 \approx 5(1.673) + 1 \approx 8.4 \) thousand dollars

The small difference is due to rounding \( t \) to one decimal place. Desmos's more precise value \( t \approx 2.753 \) gives a closer match. This is expected when working with rounded graphical solutions.

Solution:   \( t \approx 2.8 \) months,   revenue and cost \( \approx \$8{,}600 \)

Interpretation: The streaming service reaches its break-even point approximately 2.8 months after launch — roughly midway through the third month. At that point, both monthly revenue and monthly operating cost are approximately $8,600. Before this point, operating costs exceed revenue, and the service is running at a loss. After this point, revenue surpasses cost, and the service becomes profitable. Because the revenue follows a square root model \( R = 5\sqrt{t} + 1 \), it grows quickly at first and then more slowly, meaning the service must keep costs in check to remain profitable as the linear cost continues rising.



Conclusion

In this lesson, you learned how to use Desmos to graphically solve a system consisting of a linear equation and a nonlinear equation. You saw that the solution to such a system is the set of all intersection points — ordered pairs \( (x, y) \) where both graphs share the same value simultaneously. Depending on the type of nonlinear function involved (quadratic, cubic, square root, cube root, or absolute value), the number of intersection points can range from zero to several, and the graph gives you an immediate visual preview of how many solutions to expect. You practiced reading intersection coordinates directly from Desmos and verifying them algebraically by substituting back into both original equations. Finally, you applied these skills to real-world models — a skateboarder's flight path and a business break-even analysis — connecting the intersection point to meaningful, interpretable conclusions with appropriate units. Graphical methods are a powerful first step: they show you where solutions are, how many there are, and whether the answers make sense in context.
