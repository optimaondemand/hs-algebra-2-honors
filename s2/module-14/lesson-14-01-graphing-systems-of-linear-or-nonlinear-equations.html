📚 VOCABULARY

Axis of Symmetry: The vertical line that divides a parabola into two mirror-image halves, passing through the vertex and written in the form \( x = h \).

Consistent System: A system of equations that has at least one solution, meaning the graphs of the equations intersect at one or more points.

Inconsistent System: A system of equations that has no solution, meaning the graphs of the equations do not intersect at any point.

Intersection Point: An ordered pair \( (x, y) \) where two graphs cross or meet, representing a value that satisfies both equations simultaneously.

Linear Equation: An equation whose graph is a straight line, written in the form \( y = mx + b \), where \( m \) is the slope and \( b \) is the \( y \)-intercept.

No Solution: The condition of a system of equations in which the graphs do not intersect, indicating there is no ordered pair that satisfies both equations at the same time.

Parabola: The U-shaped curve that is the graph of a quadratic equation, which opens upward when \( a > 0 \) and downward when \( a < 0 \).

Quadratic Equation: An equation of degree two in the form \( y = ax^2 + bx + c \), where \( a \neq 0 \), whose graph is a parabola.

Solution of a System: An ordered pair \( (x, y) \) that satisfies all equations in the system simultaneously and corresponds to a point of intersection on the graph.

Solution Set: The collection of all ordered pairs \( (x, y) \) that satisfy every equation in a system, which may contain one solution, more than one solution, or no solution.

System of Equations: A set of two or more equations with the same variables, considered together to find values that satisfy all equations simultaneously.

Vertex: The highest or lowest point on a parabola, written as the ordered pair \( (h, k) \), where the axis of symmetry intersects the curve.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

In today's lesson, we will explore how to solve systems of equations using graphs. A system of equations is a set of two or more equations that share the same variables. Solving a system means finding the value or values that satisfy all equations in the system at the same time. Graphically, each equation produces a curve in the coordinate plane, and the solution to the system is any point where those curves intersect — because an intersection point lies on both graphs simultaneously. In this lesson, you will first work with systems of two linear equations, whose graphs are straight lines, and then move on to systems of two quadratic equations, whose graphs are parabolas. For each type, you will learn how many solutions are possible, how to identify solutions from a graph, and how to verify that a point is a true solution by substituting back into both equations.

Solving Systems of Two Linear Equations Graphically

When you graph a linear equation in two variables, the result is a straight line. A system of two linear equations, therefore, produces two lines in the same coordinate plane. The solution of the system is the ordered pair \( (x, y) \) that satisfies both equations — graphically, that is the point where the two lines cross.

To solve a system graphically, graph both equations on the same coordinate plane and locate the point or points of intersection. Because two distinct lines in the plane can relate to each other in only three possible ways, there are exactly three possible outcomes for a system of two linear equations.

Three Possible Solution Cases for a System of Two Linear Equations

One Solution:  The two lines have different slopes. They intersect at exactly one point. That ordered pair is the unique solution to the system.

No Solution:  The two lines have the same slope but different y-intercepts — they are parallel. Parallel lines never intersect, so no ordered pair satisfies both equations at once.

Infinitely Many Solutions:  Both equations represent the exact same line — they are coincident. Every point on the line is a solution. This occurs when one equation is a constant multiple of the other (for example, \( y = 2x + 1 \) and \( 2y = 4x + 2 \) describe the same line).


Solve the System Graphically

Solve.

Step 1 — Graph both equations on the same coordinate plane.

You can use Desmos to graph each equation. Add each equation to the input lines and hit enter. Then you can hover over the point where the lines intersect and click the table icon to add the point to the graph and read its coordinates. This will represent the solution to the system.

Step 2 — Identify the intersection point. The two lines cross at one point. Reading the coordinates from the graph, the intersection appears to be at \( (2, 4) \).

Step 3 — Verify the solution by substituting \( x = 2 \) into both original equations.

\( f(2) = 2 + 2 = 4 \) ✓    \( g(2) = -2 + 6 = 4 \) ✓

Both equations produce \( y = 4 \) when \( x = 2 \), confirming \( (2, 4) \) is the solution.

Answer: \( (2,4) \) — the two lines intersect at one point, so there is one solution.

Example 1

Solve the system by graphing.

\( \begin{cases} 2x - y = 2 \\ -2x + 3y = 8 \\ \end{cases} \)

Enter both equations into Desmos. Identify the intersection point from the graph and verify your answer by substituting into both equations.

Show Solution

Creating Graph:

Add both equations to the input fields in Desmos in their given forms. Click on the intersection point and add it to the graph.

Reading the graph: The two lines intersect at \( (3.5, 5) \).

Verify in \( 2x - y = 2 \):   \( 2(3.5) - 5 = 7 - 5 = 2 \) ✓

Verify in \( -2x + 3y = 8 \):   \( -2(3.5) + 3(5) = -7 +15 = 8 \) ✓

Answer:   \( \{(3.5, 5)\} \) — one solution; the lines have different slopes and intersect at exactly one point.



Example 2

Solve the system by graphing.

\( \begin{cases} y - 3 = 3(x + 1) \\ 6x - 2y = 4 \\ \end{cases} \)

Enter both equations into Desmos as written — Desmos accepts point-slope and standard form directly. Examine the graph carefully and identify any solution(s), if they exist.

Show Solution

Creating the graph:

Add both equations to the input fields in Desmos in their given forms. Click on the intersection point and add it to the graph.

Reading the graph: The two lines run in the same direction with the same steepness and never cross — they are parallel.

Both lines have slope \( 3 \) but different y-intercepts. Parallel lines never cross.

Algebraic confirmation: \( 3x + 6 = 3x - 2 \Rightarrow 6 = -2 \). False — no solution.

Answer:   No Solution, — the lines are parallel with slope \( 3 \) but different y-intercepts, so they never intersect.



Solving Systems of Two Quadratic Equations Graphically

When both equations in a system are quadratic, each graph is a parabola. Just as with linear systems, the solution to a system of two quadratic equations is the ordered pair or pairs where both graphs share the same point — the intersection points. The process is the same: graph both parabolas on the same coordinate plane, identify the intersection point(s), and verify each solution by substituting back into both equations.

The key difference from linear systems is the number of solutions that are possible. Because subtracting one quadratic equation from the other produces a new quadratic equation, and a quadratic equation can have at most two real solutions, a system of two quadratic equations can have at most two solutions. However, the parabolas might not intersect at all, or they might just touch at one point. This gives three possible solution cases.

Three Possible Solution Cases for a System of Two Quadratic Equations

Two Solutions: \(\{(x1,y1), (x2,y2)\}\) The parabolas cross each other at two distinct points. This is the most common case. The two intersection points are both solutions to the system.

One Solution: \(\{(x,y)\}\) The parabolas are tangent — they touch at exactly one point without crossing. The single point of tangency is the solution.

No Solution: \(\varnothing \) The parabolas do not intersect at all. Setting the two equations equal produces a quadratic with a negative discriminant, meaning no real values of \( x \) satisfy both equations simultaneously.



Solve the system graphically:  

\( \begin{cases} y= x^2 - 2x - 3 \\ y = -(x-1)^2 + 4 \\ \end{cases} \)

Step 1 — Identify each parabola. \( y1 = x^2 - 2x - 3 \) is in standard form — it opens upward with vertex \( (1, -4) \). \( y2 = -(x-1)^2 + 4 \) is in vertex form — it opens downward with vertex \( (1, 4) \).

Step 2 — Graph both equations on the same coordinate plane using Desmos.

Step 3 — Identify the intersection points. Reading the graph, the two parabolas cross at \( (-1, 0) \) and \( (3, 0) \) — both on the x-axis.

Step 4 — Verify both solutions.

For \( (-1, 0) \):   \( f(-1) = 1 + 2 - 3 = 0 \) ✓    \( g(-1) = -(-2)^2 + 4 = -4 + 4 = 0 \) ✓

For \( (3, 0) \):   \( f(3) = 9 - 6 - 3 = 0 \) ✓    \( g(3) = -(2)^2 + 4 = -4 + 4 = 0 \) ✓


Answer: \( \{(-1,\ 0),(3,\ 0)\} \) — two solutions; the parabolas intersect at two distinct points.

Example 3

Solve the system by graphing.

\( \begin{cases} y = (x - 3)^2 - 4 \\ y = -x^2 + 5 \\ \end{cases} \)

Enter both equations into Desmos. Identify all intersection points and verify by substituting into both equations.

Show Solution

Create the graph: Add each equation into the input lines in Desmos.

Reading the graph: \( f(x) \) is in vertex form — vertex \( (3, -4) \), opens upward. \( g(x) \) is in standard form — vertex \( (0, 5) \), opens downward. The parabolas cross at \( (0, 5) \) and \( (3, -4) \).

Verify \( (0, 5) \):   \( f(0) = (0-3)^2-4 = 9-4 = 5 \) ✓    \( g(0) = -0+5 = 5 \) ✓

Verify \( (3, -4) \):   \( f(3) = (3-3)^2-4 = -4 \) ✓    \( g(3) = -9+5 = -4 \) ✓

Algebraic check: \( (x-3)^2-4 = -x^2+5 \Rightarrow x^2-6x+9-4 = -x^2+5 \Rightarrow 2x^2-6x = 0 \Rightarrow 2x(x-3) = 0 \Rightarrow x=0 \) or \( x=3 \) ✓

Answer:   \( \{(0,\ 5),(3,\ -4)\} \) — two solutions.



Example 4

Solve the system by graphing.

\( \begin{cases} y = x^2 - 2x + 3 \\ y = -(x - 1)^2 + 2 \\ \end{cases} \)

Enter both equations into Desmos. Pay close attention to where and how the two parabolas meet.

Show Solution

Create the graph: Add each equation into the input lines in Desmos.

Reading the graph: \( f(x) \) is in standard form and opens upward. \( g(x) \) is in vertex form and opens downward. Both have a vertex at \( (1, 2) \) and the curves touch at that single point without crossing — this is a tangent intersection.

Verify \( (1, 2) \):   \( f(1) = 1-2+3 = 2 \) ✓    \( g(1) = -(0)+2 = 2 \) ✓

Algebraic check: \( x^2-2x+3 = -(x-1)^2+2 \Rightarrow x^2-2x+3 = -x^2+2x-1+2 \Rightarrow 2x^2-4x+2 = 0 \Rightarrow (x-1)^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x=1 \) (double root — one solution) ✓

Answer:   \( \{(1,\ 2)\} \) — one solution; the parabolas are tangent and touch at exactly one point.



Example 5

Solve the system by graphing.

\( \begin{cases} y = (x + 1)^2 + 2 \\ y = -x^2 - 1 \\ \end{cases} \)

Enter both equations into Desmos. Examine the graph carefully and identify any solution(s), if they exist.

Show Solution

Create the graph: Add each equation into the input lines in Desmos.

Reading the graph: \( f(x) \) is in vertex form — vertex \( (-1, 2) \), opens upward, sitting entirely above the x-axis. \( g(x) \) is in standard form — vertex \( (0, -1) \), opens downward, sitting entirely below the x-axis. There is a visible gap between the two curves; they never meet.

Why no intersection? The minimum value of \( f(x) \) is \( 2 \) at the vertex \( (-1, 2) \). The maximum value of \( g(x) \) is \( -1 \) at the vertex \( (0, -1) \). Since \( 2 > -1 \), the lowest point of \( f \) is always higher than the highest point of \( g \).

Algebraic confirmation: \( (x+1)^2+2 = -x^2-1 \Rightarrow x^2+2x+1+2 = -x^2-1 \Rightarrow 2x^2+2x+4 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2+x+2 = 0 \). Discriminant: \( 1 - 8 = -7 < 0 \). No real solutions. ✓

Answer:   — the parabolas do not intersect. The minimum of \( f(x) \) is \( 2 \), which is greater than the maximum of \( g(x) \) at \( -1 \).



Conclusion

In this lesson, you learned how to solve systems of equations graphically by locating the points where two curves share the same coordinates. For systems of two linear equations, you found that the two lines can intersect at one point, run parallel with no intersection, or coincide with infinitely many solutions — and the slopes of the lines determine which case applies. For systems of two quadratic equations, both graphs are parabolas, and because setting two quadratics equal produces another quadratic, there can be at most two real solutions. The two parabolas can intersect at two distinct points, touch at exactly one tangent point, or have no points of intersection at all. You also saw that equations can appear in different forms — standard form, vertex form, point-slope, and standard form for linear — and Desmos accepts all of them directly, so you can graph any system without converting first. In every case, reading the graph gives you the solution candidates, and substituting back into both equations confirms whether those points are true solutions. These graphical methods give you a powerful visual tool for understanding what a solution to a system actually means — a point that satisfies every equation in the system simultaneously — and that understanding carries forward into algebraic methods for solving systems that you will encounter in more complex settings throughout the course.
