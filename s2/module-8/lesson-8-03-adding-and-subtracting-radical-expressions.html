📚 VOCABULARY

Coefficient: A numerical factor multiplied by a variable, radical, or algebraic term.

Equivalent radical expressions: Radical expressions that may look different but have the same simplified value for all allowable values of the variables.

Like radical terms: Radical expressions that have the same index and the same radicand, allowing them to be combined through addition or subtraction.

Radical: An expression that uses the radical symbol ​ to represent a root of a number or algebraic expression.

Radicand: The number, variable, or algebraic expression inside a radical symbol.

Simplify: To rewrite an expression in its most reduced or efficient form while keeping the value unchanged.

Unlike radical terms: Radical expressions that do not share the same index and radicand, and cannot be added or subtracted unless they are first simplified into like terms.

Variable expression: An algebraic expression containing one or more variables, numbers, and operations.

💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Radicals appear frequently in Algebra 2 when solving equations, working with quadratic functions, and simplifying expressions. Just like combining like terms in algebra, you can combine radical expressions through addition and subtraction—but only when certain conditions are met. Understanding when radicals can be combined and how to simplify them when they cannot be combined initially is essential. In this lesson, you will learn how to recognize like radicals, how to combine them, and how to simplify radicals so that unlike expressions can become like radicals. You will work through examples and practice problems to strengthen your skills.

Adding and Subtracting Like Radical Terms

Just as algebraic expressions such as \(3x+5x\) can be combined because both terms contain the same variable \(x\), radical expressions can be combined only when they have the same index and the same radicand.

These are called like radical terms.

Key Principle

You may add or subtract radicals only when they match exactly in index and radicand:

In this case, the radical part stays the same; only the coefficients are added or subtracted.

Example 1

Find the sum.

\(3\sqrt{5}+7\sqrt{5}\)

Solution:

We begin by checking that the radicands are the same. Since both radicands are 5, then we can combine the coefficients, leaving the radicand as is.

Answer: \(10\sqrt{5}\)



Example 2

Find the difference.

\(8\sqrt{2}-9\sqrt{2}\)

Solution:

We begin by checking that the radicands are the same. Since both radicands are 2, then we can combine the coefficients, leaving the radicand as is.

Answer: \(-\sqrt{2}\)



Example 3

Find the sum.

\(5\sqrt[3]{y}+3\sqrt[3]{y}\)

Solution:

We begin by checking that the radicands are the same. Since both radicands are y, then we can combine the coefficients, leaving the radicand as is.

Answer: \(8\sqrt[3]{y}\)



Example 4

Generate an equivalent expression.

\(\sqrt[4]{3xy}+5\sqrt[4]{3xy}-4\sqrt[4]{3xy}\)

Solution:

We begin by checking that the radicands are the same. Since both radicands are 3xy, then we can combine the coefficients, leaving the radicand as is.

Answer: \(2\sqrt[4]{3xy}\)

Adding and Subtracting Radicals with Unlike Radicands

When radical expressions have different radicands or indices, they are unlike radical terms and cannot be combined—unless you simplify the radicals first.

Many radicands contain perfect square or perfect cube factors that can be pulled out. Once simplified, the expressions may turn into like radicals.


Steps for Combining Unlike Radicals

  1. Factor the radicands and simplify each radical.

    • Pull out perfect powers that match the index.

    • Rewrite the expression as a product of a simplified radical and a coefficient.

  2. Check if the simplified radicals now match.

  3. Combine like radicals using addition or subtraction.

Example 1

Generate an equivalent expression.

\(\sqrt{45}+3\sqrt{20}\)

Solution:

We begin by checking that the radicands are the same.

The radicands are different. Therefore, we need to simplify the radicands to see if they are like.

Once we simplify the radicals, the expression becomes \(3\sqrt{5}+6\sqrt{5}\).

Now that the radicands are the same, we can combine the coefficients.

Answer: \(9\sqrt{5}\)



Example 2

Generate an equivalent expression.

\(6\sqrt{12}-2\sqrt{27}\)

Solution:

We begin by checking that the radicands are the same.

The radicands are different. Therefore, we need to simplify the radicands to see if they are like.

Once we simplify the radicals, the expression becomes \(12\sqrt{3}-6\sqrt{3}\).

Now that the radicands are the same, we can combine the coefficients.

Answer: \(6\sqrt{3}\)



Example 3

Generate an equivalent expression.

\(\sqrt[3]{54n^5}-\sqrt[3]{16n^5}\)

Solution:

We begin by checking that the radicands are the same.

The radicands are different. Therefore, we need to simplify the radicands to see if they are like.

Once we simplify the radicals, the expression becomes \(3n\sqrt[3]{2n^2}-2n\sqrt[3]{2n^2}\).

Now that the radicands are the same, we can combine the coefficients.

Answer: \(n\sqrt[3]{2n^2}\)



Conclusion

Adding and subtracting radical expressions is very similar to combining like terms in algebra—radicals must match in both index and radicand. When they do, you simply combine the coefficients. When radicals do not match, simplify each expression first. Many radicals contain perfect square or perfect cube factors that reveal like terms once simplified. Mastering these skills will help you work confidently with more complex expressions, solve equations involving radicals, and prepare for advanced topics in Algebra 2 and beyond.
