💡 EXPLORING THE MATH

Radical expressions appear often in Algebra 2—whether you are solving equations, simplifying expressions, or working with functions. To successfully manipulate radical expressions, you need to understand how radicals behave under multiplication and division. Fortunately, radicals follow consistent patterns based on the product property and quotient property of radicals. In this lesson, you will learn how to multiply and divide radical expressions, work through numerical and algebraic examples, and apply these skills to write simplified, equivalent radical expressions.

Multiplying Radical Expressions

Multiplying radical expressions is often more straightforward than adding or subtracting them. When multiplying, the radicands do not need to match. The product property of radicals allows you to multiply radicands directly.

This property works for numerical radicands and for algebraic ones.

After multiplying the radicands, your next step is to simplify the radical, using factorization to pull out perfect squares, cubes, or other perfect powers depending on the index.

Dividing Radical Expressions

Dividing radical expressions uses the quotient property of radicals.

Just like multiplication, you may simplify radicals before or after applying the property. When dividing, sometimes the denominator contains a radical. In Algebra 2, it is standard to rationalize the denominator to remove radicals from the bottom of a fraction.

Example 1

Generate an equivalent expression.

Solution:

Divide the radicands under one radical.

Simplify the radicand using perfect squares.

Answer: \(5\)



Example 2

Generate an equivalent expression.

Solution:

Divide the radicands under one radical.

Simplify the radicand using perfect squares. We will also need to rationalize the denominator.

We rationalize the denominator by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the denominator. This will make a perfect square in the denominator so that there will be no radicals in the denominator.

Answer: \(\frac{4\sqrt{42}}{7}\)

Example 5

Generate an equivalent expression.

Solution:

Divide the radicands under one radical.

We begin by looking at what number 24 and -81 have in common that we can divide out of both to reduce them.

Next, we look at the division of \(a^7\) and \(a^{-2}\), using exponent properties, we subtract the exponents. So, it becomes \(a^{7-(-2)}\) which simplifies to \(a^9\) in the numerator.

Then we look at the b next. We have \(b^{-1}\) in the numerator and \(b^2\) in the denominator. So, this becomes \(b^{-1-2}=b^{-3}\). Since the exponent is negative, we put it in the denominator to make it positive.

Simplify the radicand using perfect cubes.

Answer: \(\Large -\frac{2a^3}{3b}\)



Conclusion

Multiplying and dividing radicals follow consistent rules based on the product and quotient properties. When multiplying, radicands may be combined freely under a single radical, and then simplified. When dividing, radicals can be combined or separated, but special care must be taken when a radical appears in the denominator—this is where rationalizing becomes essential. Mastering these skills helps you manipulate expressions accurately and prepares you for more advanced work with radical equations, rational exponents, and functions involving roots.
